Shade and Roof Orientation

On a treed block the layout matters more than the panel brand.

Hills · winter sun angle

A 15 metre stringybark to the north of your roof casts a shadow roughly 24 metres long at winter noon. In December the same tree throws about 4 metres. A design done off a summer satellite photo will look clean and then lose 40 to 60 percent of its winter output.

We walk every roof in person and we model the shade for 21 June, not 21 December. If part of the roof is dead in winter we say so and we do not put panels there just to hit a round number.

Panels on a string are wired in series. On a plain string inverter, one panel under a branch pulls the whole string down to its output. The fix is layout: keep shaded panels on their own string or their own MPPT input, and keep the clean north face separate.

Both GoodWe and Sigenergy inverters we install carry two or more MPPT inputs. We use them. On heavily broken shade we add optimisers on the affected panels only, not on all of them, because optimisers cost money and add a failure point on panels that never needed them.

North · west · east

A north-facing array at Adelaide latitude produces the most kilowatt hours over a year. A west-facing array produces around 12 to 15 percent less but produces it later in the afternoon, when you are home, aircon is running, and the feed-in tariff is at its worst. With a battery this matters less. Without one it matters a lot.

East-facing roofs are fine for morning loads and hot water. South-facing roofs we generally leave alone below about 10 degrees pitch unless there is nothing else.
